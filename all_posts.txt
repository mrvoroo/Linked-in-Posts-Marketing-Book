=== DAY 3 | extra | #12 | value ===
Bridge job or target role? Let your runway number decide, not your nerves.

Panic takes the first offer. A plan sets the date by which you'd take one.

The rough test (a test, not a rule):
- Plenty of months: protect the search and be selective.
- Getting short: a bridge job, contract or fractional project can be the right call. Choose it on purpose, and pick one you can leave.

=== DAY 4 | extra | #16 | value ===
Calling your lender after you miss a payment costs you options. Call before the due date.

Many lenders and card companies have hardship or payment-relief programs, but what's offered varies by company, product and state. Ask; don't assume. Waiting until you're behind can add fees and make the conversation harder.

Before the call: have your account number, balance, due date and what you can pay, even if it's less than usual.

Say: 'I was laid off on [date] and my income has changed. I want to stay current. What hardship or payment-relief options do you offer?'

Then ask what each option costs (fees, interest, end date), how it will be reported to the credit bureaus, and for the terms in writing. Note the date, the person's name and a reference number. You don't have to accept the first offer.

=== DAY 5 | morning | #17 | value ===
Your income dropped. Your student loan payment didn't. Call the servicer before you miss one.

A missed payment can turn a stressful month into a worse one. Options exist, but you have to ask for them, and they differ by loan type.

First step: find your servicer, the company you send payments to. For federal loans, the official federal student aid site lists them. For private loans, call the lender directly.

A severance agreement usually asks you to give up the right to sue, in exchange for pay or benefits. Read it as a contract, not a courtesy.

At director level and up, the package can hold more than a number: equity, bonus, PTO, non-compete and non-disparagement terms sit in the paragraphs after the amount. Signing on day one just to be done with it is a tempting move, and after you sign, asking gets a lot harder.

Page 27 of the plan, blurred in the image on purpose, is the severance cheat sheet. Three levers it points to: read the release section line by line, ask what reference the employer will give about you, and ask whether you can get more time in writing.

A lever many people miss if they're 40 or older: in a group layoff, the employer must also tell you the job titles and ages of everyone chosen and not chosen (page 10). You generally get at least 21 days to consider the agreement (45 in a group layoff) and 7 days to revoke after signing. Under 40, federal law sets no minimum review time, so ask for at least a week, in writing.

One panic move can cost you thousands after a layoff: cashing out your 401(k).

Your 401(k) belongs to you. But cashing out early counts as income that year, and under age 59 and a half you often owe an extra 10% tax. A check made out to you has 20% withheld for federal tax, and you must replace that 20% from other money within 60 days to roll over the full amount.

The plan's rule of thumb: withdrawing retirement money should be the last tool, after budget cuts, unemployment and lender hardship programs. Take a week before deciding, and talk to a tax professional first. This is general information, not tax advice.
